Customizable Homepage: Usability Findings

Tested with 5 GLM clients in an unmoderated remote study (Userlytics). August 2026.

5 / 5 Recruitment target met
4.6 Customize likelihood (of 5)
100% Daily GLM users

Customization is validated. The risk has moved to what the modules do once they are added.

Every participant predicted what Customize did before clicking it, and every participant added modules without help. Nobody struggled with the mechanics. What they attacked was scope, filtering, and provenance inside the modules, plus the words we used to describe them.

What we learned

Counts are out of 5 participants. Confidence tiers are defined in the methodology appendix.

On the sparse default

The default state was deliberately kept close to today's page so current users would not read the release as a wholesale change. Two of five still described it as bare or too small, and the smaller design system type scale is part of that. The continuity strategy is sound, but it does not carry itself. The first run customization prompt and the old and new toggle are load bearing parts of the release, not conveniences.

This also strengthens the case already in discussion with solutions engineering: the demo and new client default should be a fully configured, visually dense page. The sparse default is a change management artifact for existing clients and should not be the surface a prospect ever sees.

Internal input, not a study finding: solutions engineering and implementation want customization for their own use as well as the client's, to run better demos and to stand up new clients in a configured state rather than an empty one. That is a second audience for the same capability and it is worth naming when this work gets scoped, because it changes who the first users are.

What to do next

Ship with confidence

  • Fixed Workload module at the top, in the linear left to right process reading
  • Customize as the entry label and the toolbar model behind it (5/5 predicted it correctly)
  • Add, remove, and row creation with 1, 2, and 3 column rows
  • Per user customization as the ownership default (3/5 self only, 2/5 both, 0/5 for others)
  • Needs Your Attention as a default on module
  • Foundation Pulse (three advocates, no detractors)
  • Old and new toggle plus an upfront customization pass for existing clients

Iterate before shipping

  • Rename bento to widget in all user facing copy, including the panel, the toolbar, and the module registry. Widget matches GivingData, which lets sales and marketing use one word for customization across both products. (3/5)
  • Replace Workload with a plainer term on the fixed module. The organization of it reads correctly, the label does not. (1/5 blocked, and the term is internal in origin)
  • Add drag between rows and columns. Both friction moments in the study happened here: one participant tried to move a widget across and could not, another grabbed a resize handle expecting a move. (2/5)
  • Add per module scope controls to every metrics module, at minimum process and date range. Without them the metrics are decoration. (3/5 raised, 2/5 blocking)
  • Say whose work Shortcuts covers. Mine or the whole foundation's. One participant declined it purely because she could not tell, and said she would use it if it were hers. (1/5, cheap to fix)
  • Hide unused processes in the fixed Workload row. Config driven from enabled processes, not user editable. Three participants asked to remove LOI or Evaluations. (3/5)
  • Upcoming Installments specifically was the only chart anyone asked to be a list. Worth a look because the same content shown as a sentence inside Needs Your Attention drew the strongest reactions in the study, including from the participant who does all payment tracking herself. (1/5, do not generalize past this widget)
  • Expose provenance and thresholds. Where focus area and location come from, and how the interaction gap and overdue windows are set. (3/5)
  • Revisit Foundation Impact naming so it does not promise outcomes it cannot show. (1/5)
  • Check density and type scale on the default state at standard zoom. Two participants read the page as small or bare. (2/5)

Test separately

  • Program Officers as their own round. Two of five sat on that side, the module set was designed around the Grants Manager, and My Queue, My Evaluations, My Follow Ups, and Active Work all live or die on their input. One participant asked for this directly.
  • The global process filter against real process structures. One participant duplicates processes per cycle and per focus area, so the filter does not slice her data. Validate whether that is common before treating filtering as solved. This may be a data model question, not a UI one.
  • Supervisory visibility. Whether team progress belongs on the homepage, in a report, or in a separate view.
  • Geographic granularity for funding by location. County and city were both named. Confirm the source field before building.
  • Demo configuration with solutions engineering. A fully configured layout is a separate artifact from the client default and should be validated on the demo axis, not with existing users.
  • Free form layout is set aside, not deferred. The parallel Gridstack exploration is closed for this release. The friction the study found was localized to moving widgets between rows and columns, which a targeted fix addresses. Nobody asked for pixel level placement, and layout looks like a set once decision, so a grid rewrite would buy precision this audience has not asked for.

Post ship signal (not yet agreed)

Candidates for a team conversation, grouped by the three things this investment is meant to do. Nothing here is committed. The one point that holds regardless of what we choose: most of these need a pre launch baseline, so the measurement decision has to happen before release, not after.

  • Win more deals. Whether SEs now feature the homepage in a demo rather than passing through it, which is what Gong transcripts show today. Then demo to next step conversion, and whether UI and UX keeps coding as the top loss reason. ASP sits too far downstream to attribute to this page.
  • Give clients flexibility. Share of accounts with at least one modified layout. Which widgets get added, in what order, and which get removed, since that is the best input to the next registry release. Whether editing recurs after week one, which is the direct test of the set once question. Widgets per user, since one participant asked whether there is a cap.
  • Reduce the friction that drives people out of GLM. Whether homepage to reporting navigation declines, and whether the homepage self loop in the path data shrinks. Ticket volume on how to use and reporting inquiry.
  • Modernization. Load performance on the new page and whether it shipped without the UI heavy slip pattern. This is a delivery measure rather than a user signal, and should be owned by engineering rather than folded into product adoption.
  • Change management. How many existing clients switch back to the old page, and whether they come back. This is the cheapest early warning we will have.
  • Role segmentation across all of the above once volume allows it, since role was the strongest divider in this sample.

The evidence behind the top findings

Quotes are lightly cleaned for readability. Clip links are placeholders pending Userlytics export.

Validated (4/5)

Customization mechanics are not the problem

All five participants correctly predicted what the Customize button would do before clicking it, describing it as turning things off, changing what is on the page, or moving boxes around. All five then added modules, created rows, and changed column counts without assistance. Stumbles were brief and self resolved, most often a moment of hunting for the row or column control before finding it.

That result frees the remaining effort. The open questions in this release are about module content, naming, and scope, not about whether people can operate the editor.

"Hopefully it allows me to take off boxes or change colors or get rid of things that I don't care about or don't use."

Convergent (3/5, 2 blocking)

A metric you cannot scope is decoration

This was the sharpest criticism in the study and the one we did not anticipate. Metrics modules render a number or a chart for the whole book of business with no way to narrow it. One participant tested the global process filter against her own configuration, watched the numbers stay put, and concluded the module would not be useful to her. Another rejected chart as the presentation format for list shaped data, wanting a list or a single number instead. A third wanted the interaction gap threshold configurable to her foundation's two to three year site visit cadence.

The pattern is the same one described in the workload grid problem statement. Staff leave GLM to answer portfolio questions because the in product view cannot be sliced. Putting an unfilterable metric on the homepage reproduces that dead end one screen earlier.

"I don't know why you guys try to give us things without filters. It would actually not be useful to me without being able to filter things out."

Validated (4/5)

Needs Your Attention is the anchor module

Four of five named it as a module they would add, and it produced the strongest reactions in the study. It won both direct comparisons against My Queue. One participant wanted it repositioned closer to the top of the page, which is a useful signal about default placement rather than a criticism.

The follow up question it triggers is configuration. Two participants immediately asked how the thresholds are set: how the system knows an interaction is overdue, and whether the day counts can be tuned. The module earns its place, and it needs a visible answer to how it decides what needs attention.

"Oh my God, needs your attention. That gives you actual information. That's really nice. I would want that actually closer to the top."

Emerging (2/5, high strategic weight)

The homepage displaced report pulling, unprompted

One participant described the funding by focus area module as the thing she recreates by hand for board meetings, and said she currently has to pull a full fiscal year report, find it, run it, and analyze it to get numbers the module shows on login. A second builds the equivalent map herself in ArcGIS today. Neither was asked about reporting.

This is the reporting friction retention signal answered by a homepage module, in a client's own words. It is also the most usable demo asset the study produced, because it describes a before and after rather than a feature. Worth noting the constraint: this only holds if the modules can be scoped, which is the finding above.

"Funding by focus area, whoa, that is a game changer. This is literally stuff we could screenshot, and this is the stuff we recreate for our board meetings."

Convergent (3/5)

Our vocabulary is costing comprehension in two places

Three participants stopped on the word bento. Two had never heard it and asked what it meant. One understood it and objected anyway, calling it too cute for the product. Internal shorthand has leaked into the interface at the exact moment we are asking someone to learn a new capability. The fix is to adopt widget, which matches GivingData and lets sales and marketing describe customization the same way across both products.

Workload has a narrower version of the same problem. The module's organization reads correctly and participants moved through it without help, but one could not parse the label itself and had to translate it to dashboard before she could continue. The structure is right and the word is ours, not theirs.

Separately, and not a naming problem, the Shortcuts widget does not declare its scope. One participant declined to add it purely because she could not tell whether it covered her assignments or the whole foundation's, and said outright that she would use it if it were hers. That is a label away from being a wanted widget.

"Why is it called a bento instead of a card? I get it, it's cute, but it's a little too cute."

Emerging (2/5)

They want visibility into other people's work, not control of other people's pages

Nobody chose the option to customize someone else's homepage. But two participants described a supervisory need in the same breath: one tracks whether her program officers have completed their steps in the workflow, another flagged Active Work by Process as useful for her supervisors. A third wondered aloud what her program officers would put on their own pages.

These are two different features and we currently have neither. Configuring another person's layout is a permissions feature nobody asked for. Seeing where colleagues stand in their work is a module we have not designed. The distinction matters because the poll result could easily be read as closing the question when it only closes half of it.

"As grants manager, I also have to track how our program officers are doing. Being able to see where they are in their workflows is also helpful."

Open question

The self report and the reasoning disagree about how often people will customize

The poll returned 4.6 of 5 on likelihood of regular use, with three of five choosing Frequently. The transcripts describe something different. Asked what they would want to change once grant cycles moved on, three participants described a stable layout rather than a seasonal one. One said explicitly that she would put what matters most at the top and what she checks rarely at the bottom, and would not rearrange it each cycle. Another expected the numbers to move, not the layout.

The most likely reading is that customization is a high enthusiasm setup event with occasional tinkering afterward, not a weekly habit. The 4.6 is probably measuring appetite rather than frequency. If that holds, two things follow. First, the first run experience carries more weight than the efficiency of repeat editing, which supports the upfront customization pass already planned for launch. Second, the payback on free form layout precision shrinks, because precision placement pays off in proportion to how often someone rearranges. That is a tradeoff for engineering leadership to weigh, not a design recommendation.

Customize open rate over the first twelve weeks settles this cheaply. It should be instrumented before launch rather than after.

"Knowing me, I wouldn't want to have to keep changing this, so I would design this in a way where what's most important is up top and what I would check more infrequently is at the bottom. I wouldn't change this around each grant cycle, but I would customize it to work for me year round."

Reference

Study design

Unmoderated remote usability study conducted through Userlytics. Five GLM clients completed the study against a recruitment target of five. Participants were recruited through a Gainsight PX in app intercept to current GLM clients with no formal screener, so the sample is a wide net rather than a segmented one.

Participants saw a single screen prototype of the redesigned homepage in its default state: fixed Workload module, Needs Your Attention, Funding by focus area, a persistent process filter in the page header, and a Customize entry point. Activities moved from unaided first impressions, through predicting and then using Customize, to module preference, gaps, and comparison against the current homepage. Two self report polls closed the session on customization ownership and likelihood of use.

Confidence thresholds (n=5)

  • Validated (4 to 5 of 5): Act on now.
  • Convergent (3 of 5): Act on now where the fix is cheap, otherwise document with a named owner.
  • Emerging (2 of 5): Document and watch. Weight up where the signal carries strategic consequence.
  • Isolated (1 of 5): Note, do not act on the count alone. Several items in this readout are marked 1 of 5 and recommended anyway because the underlying design principle stands on its own.

Participants

Five participants, referred to as P1 through P5. The participant key is held in the Userlytics project. Composition from the intake polls: 3 Grants Manager or Grants Administrator, 2 Program Officer. All five use GLM daily. Four have used GLM for more than two years, one between six months and two years.

Most frequent tasks after logging in: checking application status (4 of 5), checking LOI status (3 of 5), checking follow up status (3 of 5), metrics dashboard (2 of 5), my follow ups (2 of 5). Write in responses named email communications and notes, looking up grantee and organization profiles to verify information, and searching for an organization.

Limitations

  • n=5. The target was met, but at this size a single participant moves a finding by 20 points. Counts are directional and should not be quoted as rates.
  • Role attribution per participant could not be resolved from the transcripts, so no finding in this readout is segmented by role. Given how strongly role divided module preference, that is the most consequential gap in the study.
  • One transcript was returned incomplete by Userlytics. The session itself was complete, but the later activities are not available in text, so that participant is under counted on module ranking and on both closing polls.
  • The default state was intentionally kept close to the current homepage so participants would not read the prototype as a wholesale redesign. That choice was correct for change management and it means the study says nothing about how a fully configured page performs, including in a demo.
  • Single screen prototype with no persistence. Nothing here speaks to whether a saved layout holds, syncs, or survives a session.
  • Participants were instructed to customize. First run discovery without a prompt was not tested, and that is the moment the launch plan depends on.
  • Seeded data did not match participants' real configurations. Several do not run LOIs or evaluations, one duplicates processes per cycle, and one wanted geographic data at a granularity the seed did not carry. Some reactions are about the seed, not the design.
  • Two participants hit friction with the Userlytics interface itself, including trouble advancing between activities and recovering from overlay windows. Some hesitation is platform driven.
  • No difficulty ratings or task time measures collected.

Email history with open tracking, viewable by organization (2 of 5 direct)

One participant raised this twice, unprompted, as the thing she digs for today: whether a grantee opened a reminder about a due application or follow up, and she needs it by organization because user to organization mapping is not obvious. Another named email communications and notes in a write in. No module in the registry covers it. This is the clearest gap the study found.

Assigning work from the homepage (2 of 5)

One participant asked twice for a way to assign follow up forms and tasks to a coworker, and framed it as the main reason she would open a Decisions view. Another liked assigning a site visit write up because her foundation visits organizations year round and forgets the write ups. Both are actions from the homepage rather than navigation targets, which is a different interaction model than the current module set assumes.

Annual budget remaining (1 of 5)

No module shows how much of an annual or focus area budget is left. Raised alongside a wish that Foundation Impact connected to actual outcomes. Both point at the Programs structure work rather than at this release.

Foundation Impact naming overpromises (1 of 5)

One participant said the title implies something larger than dollars awarded and grant counts, and she would leave the module off for that reason while three others named the same numbers as a top pick. The content has demand. The label sets the wrong expectation.

Geographic granularity for funding by location (3 of 5 touched)

County and city were both named as the level that would make the module useful. One participant asked what field it draws from, guessing postal code or city. Another already builds the equivalent map in ArcGIS. Worth confirming the source field before building, because the module is wanted and the granularity determines whether it is used.

Redundancy between Active Work by Process and the Workload module (1 of 5)

One participant read the two as the same information and could not reconcile the counts between them, asking what the numbers were supposed to match. If two modules present overlapping data with different scoping rules, that needs resolving before both ship as options.

Where the editor got fussy (2 of 5)

Both friction moments in the study happened at the same place: moving a widget between rows and columns. One participant read the instruction to drag the row grip to reorder, tried it, and narrated that she could not do it there. Another grabbed a resize handle expecting a move, said "oh no, that's making it bigger," then found drag and drop a beat later and recovered. Everything else in the editor, adding, removing, setting column counts, was completed unaided by all five.

Evaluations as a page rather than a module (1 of 5)

One participant preferred navigating to a dedicated evaluations page over reading evaluations on the homepage, and noticed the nav entry was gone. Worth watching in Gainsight rather than designing around at n=1.

Turning off process stages inside the Workload module (2 of 5)

Separate from removing whole processes: one participant wanted evaluations hidden from the LOI and Application columns because her foundation does not use them. The fix is likely the same config driven approach as hiding unused processes.

Density and type scale (2 of 5)

One participant described the page as bare bones and the elements as small, comparing it to early internet. Another wished things took up more of the screen and noted things getting smaller. Both reactions are to the intentionally sparse default plus the smaller design system type scale. Worth a density pass at standard zoom.

Module count limit (1 of 5)

One participant asked directly whether every module could be added or whether there is a cap, and wanted to know before planning her layout. Whatever the answer, it should be visible in the panel.

Grantee initiated outreach flag (1 of 5)

A grantee signals they want to be contacted and it appears as a flag on the homepage, instead of emailing to hunt for the right contact. Described as something the participant wished existed in any system. Aspirational, and a genuine relationship management signal.

Change management and preview ask (1 of 5)

One participant asked for a thirty minute preview session before release so she could get it on her staff's calendars and give them time to adjust rather than having them log in to an unannounced change. Directly relevant to the planned toggle and upfront customization pass, and cheap to act on.

Totals placement in the page header (1 of 5)

One participant liked the year to date total in the greeting line but said she would not think to look up there, and expected the arithmetic to sit closer to the workload counts. Isolated, but a placement question worth watching once the page carries more modules.
